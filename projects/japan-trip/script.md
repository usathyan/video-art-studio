## 00 — Prologue
style: quiet, reflective, a father remembering; unhurried, warm, a small smile in the voice
My son Ajay enrolled for a year at Temple University Japan, in Tokyo. I flew out with him to drop him off, help him land, set up his apartment, buy him a bicycle, and then I stole a week for myself.
Why "slowing time"? Because that is the thing I actually brought back. In Japan, people take time. The conductor takes time to bow at every car before closing its doors. Thousands of people at an intersection, and it is somehow quiet.
And there was a second clock running too, a father's one. These were the last few days of Ajay under my wing. You want those days to go slow. Japan obliged.

## 01 — Day 1 · Landing, and immediately getting lost
style: wry and amused, gently self-mocking about a rough first day
Nobody tells you your first train ride in Japan is a rite of passage. We spent a while looking for a train station where there was none. It was actually a bus. Then the hotel, in an alley, unmarked, with a door code that only worked if you pressed the call button twice.
We were inside five or six hours after landing, tired from the heat and the long journey. But we found a local place and had great ramen. Day one: Japan one, itinerary zero.

## 02 — Day 2 · Eight floors of geek heaven
style: bright, curious, enjoying the memory
A morning walk to the campus, my first konbini coffee, and then the errands that actually matter: bedding, kitchen tools, groceries for his apartment. Then we figured out the subway, and rode it to Akihabara.
Yodobashi Camera is eight floors, built like an expo. If a product is electronic, or made in Japan, you can find it there. It rained, we got drenched, and dinner was gyoza with vending machine beer, which sounds like a compromise and was actually perfect.

## 03 — Day 3 · An earthquake, a thousand cats, and a samba parade
style: lively storytelling, a hush for the earthquake, then rising joy for the parade
At two in the morning my phone blasted an alarm, and a second later I felt it. I rushed out to the street. It was empty. Everyone knows not to react to such small things.
We rode the little Setagaya tram to Gotokuji, the white cat temple, birthplace of the beckoning cat. Thousands of them, left by people whose wishes came true. We bought a cat and left it at the shrine, for Ajay's good luck in Japan.
At the FamilyMart, the clerk took his time and filled in my whole shipping form, while everyone in line patiently waited. Nobody sighed. That patience is the thing I keep coming back to.
Then we bought Ajay a bicycle, and on the way home, we found ourselves in a parade.
That night, our first conveyor-belt sushi. We experienced what it feels like to be locals in Tokyo.

## 04 — Day 4 · The goodbye, and the bullet train
style: tender at the goodbye, then settling into calm as the train moves
The day starts with a coffee run to the Lawson konbini. Ajay joins, we chat for a bit, then the usual fatherly advice, then hugs. He rode over on his own wheels, in his own city now.
Tokyo Station is a sensory overload. I still managed to find the exact meal I was hunting, a Daruma ekiben. The train is quiet, silent and steady.
In Kyoto, I knelt, as much as my knees allowed, for a proper tea ceremony. And that night, a Frenchman explained Japanese sake to an Indian-American in Kyoto. That is the world I want to live in.

## 05 — Day 5 · Ten thousand gates before breakfast
style: hushed awe at dawn, then warm humor about sore feet
Up at four fifteen, for sunrise inside the Senbon Torii. It is a trek up the mountain in the early morning, when the crowds are still little. Twelve thousand steps. The fox is the messenger here, and each one holds something different: a key, a scroll, a jewel.
My slip-on shoes were a bad choice. The feet voted no on the sake district.
At dusk I took a bicycle to Gion, and got lucky. One geisha, fully dressed, hurrying down an old cobblestone alley. No photo. Some things you just watch.

## 06 — Day 6 · The town of a thousand temples
style: gentle, contemplative, with affection
A ryokan breakfast, then the stepping stones across the Kamo delta. Some of the stones are turtles. Of course they are.
At Shimogamo Shrine I happened upon a geisha rehearsal of a prayer practice, one of the most memorable things I saw all trip. No photos of that, out of respect.
I think I get the idea about Kyoto being the town of a thousand temples. There is one on every street, just like the 7-Elevens. I have to confess, I am going to miss the konbinis.

## 07 — Day 7 · Arashiyama, exactly as promised
style: slow, serene, almost whispered in the bamboo
The one day that went exactly to plan, and the finest of the trip.
Everything in Arashiyama is exactly like a model: small, functional, and cute. Even the maple leaves are a third the size of ours.
The bamboo walk. Photos don't carry the sound of it. A soft, wooden rain.

## 08 — Day 8 · A seventy-two minute delay, and a neon night
style: amused and energetic, Osaka is loud and he loves it
Everyone told me Japanese trains don't run late. Mine ran seventy-two minutes late. Everyone waited quietly. Of course.
Osaka doesn't do subtle, and I love it for that. We cooked our own takoyaki, ate kushikatsu in Janjan Yokocho, one rule, never double-dip, and watched the city from three hundred meters up. The platforms go quiet. The neon never does.

## 09 — Day 9 · The goodbye that counts
style: slowing down, tender, a catch in the voice at the goodbye, then peace
The seven thirty Shinkansen back to Tokyo, and then the best detour of the trip. Ajay met me, and we burned the spare hours in Akihabara together. One last ramen.
Then the real goodbye, at Tokyo Station. And the monorail to Haneda, alone.
Each time the conductor walks past a car, they bow before closing its doors. People bow when they approach the empty seat next to you, before they sit.

## 10 — Where the ten days went
style: warm, matter-of-fact, a little proud
Ten days. Three cities. Twelve thousand steps up Fushimi Inari. One earthquake at two in the morning. And nine hundred and sixty-nine photos and videos.

## 11 — What Japan taught me
style: reflective and sincere, slow, the emotional close; end softly
What struck me most was the attitude of people. The optometrist, the drugstore saleswoman, the FamilyMart clerk. It just felt like people take time to give each other space. A true sign of respect, a sort of recognizing that everyone is important.
The country won't let you feel like a stranger for long.
I have no heavy heart leaving. And I think Ajay will have a great life here. That was the point of the whole trip, after all.
Mata kimasu. I'll be back.
